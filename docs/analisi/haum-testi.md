Look for the small basket of flowers held by the daughter; if you peer closely, you will find the artist's signature hidden there.

This painting is shrouded in mystery. We don't know who these people are, nor do we know if they paid for the portrait or if Rembrandt painted them freely. For centuries, the identity of the sitters caused great debate. Until the mid-19th century, many believed this was actually a portrait of Rembrandt and his own family. Given that the artist suffered devastating personal losses—including his wife and children—could this happy scene be a poignant "counter-idyll," a painted dream of a family life he no longer had?

Even the art world couldn't agree on its value. While some early critics dismissed the work as "strange" or a sign of the artist's decline, others, like painter Lovis Corinth, hailed it as a "Brunswick jewel." ### Professional/Hobbyist

**EN** (233 parole)

Executed between 1665 and 1668, this late work is a study in tactile surface and psychological intimacy. Note the aggressive application of paint; Rembrandt employs thick impasto and a palette knife to create a relief-like texture, particularly in the figures of the mother and youngest child. This coarse brushwork, combined with a palette dominated by browns, greens, and intense reds, marks a departure from the polished finishes of earlier eras.

Compositionally, Rembrandt strips away all representative elements. There is no furniture or architectural detail to define the space, focusing the viewer entirely on the familial interaction. While the painting was acquired by Duke Anton Ulrich of Brunswick-Wolfenbüttel, the identities of the sitters remain unknown. This ambiguity fuels scholarly debate: some suggest the work was a commission rejected by the patrons, while others interpret it as a counter-idyll to Rembrandt's own tragic domestic life following the deaths of his wife Saskia, partner Hendrickje Stoffels, and son Titus.

Historically, the reception of this piece has shifted dramatically. Once dismissed as evidence of declining skill, it is now championed as a masterly late work. Alongside The Jewish Bride and his final self-portraits, this painting documents Rembrandt's evolution into what some art historians call the first modern painter. The work is signed on the flower basket as Rembrandt f, though it remains undated, serving as a pivotal example of the artist's late-period exploration of form and emotion.
